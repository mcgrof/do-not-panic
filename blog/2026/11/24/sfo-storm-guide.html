    What an atmospheric river is

    A long, narrow conveyor of water vapor a mile or so above the ocean. When it runs into the California coast it gets shoved uphill and wrung out.

    1. The river. Ahead of a cold front, a fast low-level wind (the low-level jet) carries warm, wet air from the subtropics. Almost all of the water is invisible vapor in the lowest mile and a half of the atmosphere.
    2. Landfall. The plume hits the coast. Flat ground gets steady rain from the front itself.
    3. The squeeze. Mountains force the air upward. Rising air expands and cools, cool air holds less vapor, and the excess condenses and falls. This is why the Santa Cruz Mountains and Mount Tam routinely get two to four times what downtown SF gets from the same storm.
    4. Rain shadow. The air that makes it over the ridge has lost much of its water and sinks, so the lee side gets less.
    250–375 mi typical width
    1,200+ mi typical length
    ~12 Mississippis water moved by a moderate one, as vapor (math below)

    Where the "12 Mississippis" comes from

    Forecasters rate these storms by IVT, integrated vapor transport: how many kilograms of water vapor cross each meter of the river's width every second. It comes from adding up, layer by layer from the surface to about 5.5 miles up, the moisture content of the air times the wind speed times the mass of air in that layer. 

    IVT = (1/g) x SUM over layers of ( q x v x dp )

  q   = kg of water vapor per kg of air in the layer
  v   = wind speed in the layer, m/s
  dp  = pressure thickness of the layer, pascals
  dp/g = kg of air sitting over each square meter in that layer
         (pressure is the weight of the air above, per unit area)

  units: (kg/kg) x (m/s) x (kg/m2) = kg per meter per second

Step 1  moderate river: IVT = 500 kg/m/s
Step 2  width = 400 km = 400,000 m
Step 3  500 x 400,000 = 200,000,000 kg of water per second
Step 4  1 m3 of water = 1,000 kg, so 200,000 m3/s
Step 5  Mississippi at its mouth averages about 17,000 m3/s
Step 6  200,000 / 17,000 = about 12 Mississippis

    The storm scale (Ralph et al., 2019) starts from peak IVT, then shifts a category down if the river lasts under 24 hours and up if it lasts over 48:

    250–500 weak
    500–750 moderate
    750–1000 strong
    1000–1250 extreme
    1250+ exceptional

    Who figured this out

    Reginald Newell's group at MIT first described "tropospheric rivers" in 1992 after spotting filaments of moisture in global weather analyses. Yong Zhu and Newell switched to the name "atmospheric rivers" in 1994, and in 1998 showed that four or five of these filaments, covering under a tenth of the globe's circumference at mid-latitudes, carry over 90% of the poleward water vapor transport. Marty Ralph, Paul Neiman and Gary Wick flew research aircraft through them off California in the winter of 1997–98 and published the measurements in 2004, which pinned down the roughly 400 km width and the low-level jet. Ralph's 2019 paper added the five-step scale above. "Pineapple Express" is the older folk name for the ones that tap moisture near Hawaii.
