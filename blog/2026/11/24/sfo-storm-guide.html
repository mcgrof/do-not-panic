What to do before leaving

Check your booking

  • Enable airline notifications. Check the flight again before leaving home.
  • Check the inbound aircraft if available. Its arrival is useful information, but aircraft assignments can change and crew or traffic restrictions can still intervene.
  • If your plans are flexible, compare available replacement flights under the waiver. Confirm the price and eligibility before accepting a change.

Plan the trip to SFO

  • Check Caltrans for highway closures and local alerts for access streets. Never drive through floodwater or around a closure.
  • BART avoids the highway journey, but check service alerts and your first train. Thanksgiving hours are 8 AM–midnight; Friday is 5 AM–midnight. An early Thursday flight may need another way to the airport.
  • SFO recommends arriving at least 2 hours before domestic departures and 3 before international departures. Add ground travel time separately; follow your airline’s check-in deadlines.

If the airline changes the flight

  • For a cancellation or qualifying significant change, you can decline travel and credits and receive a refund for unused travel under DOT rules , including when weather is the cause.
  • The late-arrival threshold is 3+ hours for domestic itineraries or 6+ for international ones, measured against the original scheduled arrival at the destination. Other significant changes can also qualify.
  • If you take a replacement flight, you do not get a refund for that flown travel. Review the options before accepting a voucher or rebooking.

What the cancellation numbers can tell you

Today’s counts show disruptions already reported. They do not measure the chance that your flight will be canceled tomorrow. Even a current cancellation rate would need the matching number of scheduled flights, and those exact denominators were not available in the reviewed airline table. A larger airline can have more cancellations simply because it operates more flights.

Forecasting an individual flight would require its route, timing, inbound aircraft, crew availability, airport restrictions and comparable historical outcomes, with a model tested on unseen disruptions. Rain probabilities are not flight-cancellation probabilities. The weather cards are qualitative planning guidance shared across airlines, not airline-specific risk scores.

An earlier version of this guide estimated percentages from sparse storm reports and judgment calls. Those unsupported odds were removed during review. The new airline chart uses observed counts only; missing data stays unknown.

SFO explains that close runway spacing limits arrivals in poor visibility. Wind, traffic restrictions and aircraft rotations also matter. A ground stop can hold flights bound for SFO at their origin; it does not necessarily mean the airport is closed. Read the scope and timing of any FAA restriction and check your airline.

The gallons math

Step 1  1 inch of rain on 1 sq ft = 12 x 12 x 1 = 144 cubic inches
Step 2  1 gallon = 231 cubic inches, so 144 / 231 = 0.623 gallons
Step 3  1 sq mi = 5,280 x 5,280 = 27,878,400 sq ft
Step 4  Illustrative city-sized area: 46.9 x 27,878,400 = 1.31 billion sq ft
Step 5  1.31 billion x 0.623 = about 815 million gallons per inch of rain
Step 6  Reference pool = 2,500 m3 = about 660,000 gallons

This is a volume illustration: depth multiplied by area. It assumes uniform rain, not an actual citywide measured total, flood depth or runoff forecast. Absorption, drainage, storage and evaporation change how much water reaches streets and drains.

The tides: water level is not flood depth

High bay water can slow drainage through gravity-fed outfalls and push water into low shoreline areas. Pumps, barriers and local elevations affect what floods. A dry runway does not establish that every road nearby is passable.

What an atmospheric river is

A long, narrow region of strong water-vapor transport. When moist air rises over coastal mountains, it cools and can produce heavy rain or snow. The illustration below is schematic, not a forecast of this storm or a drawing to scale.

  1. The river. Strong winds carry moist air toward the coast. Much of the moisture transport is concentrated low in the atmosphere; the water vapor itself is invisible.
  2. Landfall. The plume reaches land. Frontal lifting can produce rain even away from mountains.
  3. The squeeze. Mountains force air upward. Rising air expands and cools, allowing vapor to condense into droplets or ice. This can greatly increase rainfall on the windward slopes.
  4. Rain shadow. The air that makes it over the ridge has lost much of its water and sinks, so the lee side gets less.
250–375 mi typical width
1,200+ mi typical length
~12 Mississippis illustrative vapor flow, not this storm (assumptions below)

A scale comparison: an illustrative “12 Mississippis”

Integrated vapor transport (IVT) combines moisture and horizontal wind through the atmosphere. Direction matters: add the wind components through the layers first, then take the magnitude. To turn that into a flow across an entire plume, use the transport perpendicular to a cross-section and integrate across its width. A peak IVT at one point is not the width-average. 

IVT_x = SUM(q x u x dp) / g
IVT_y = SUM(q x v x dp) / g
IVT   = sqrt(IVT_x^2 + IVT_y^2)

q = water-vapor mass / air mass (kg/kg)
u, v = horizontal wind components (m/s)
dp = layer pressure thickness (Pa); g = gravity (m/s2)
IVT units = kg of water per meter per second

Illustration only: assume a perpendicular, width-mean IVT of 500 kg/m/s
across 400 km, not a peak of 500 at one point.
500 x 400,000 = 200,000,000 kg/s of transported vapor
Divide by 1,000 kg/m3 = 200,000 m3/s liquid-water equivalent
Using about 17,000 m3/s for the Mississippi: about 12 river flows

This is a hypothetical flow calculation, not an estimate for this week. Only part of the transported vapor falls locally as precipitation. NOAA’s background on atmospheric rivers explains why comparisons with large rivers are useful.

These are IVT intensity bands in kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹. The Ralph et al. (2019) event scale also uses duration at a location and a three-hour average for peak IVT. The baseline is 24–48 hours; shorter events shift down a category and longer ones up, within the scale’s limits. These bands alone are not event categories or flight-disruption ratings:
